Two-Factor Authentication

Even a strong, unique password stored in a manager can still leak - through a data breach, a phishing site, or malware on your device. Two-factor authentication, often shortened to 2FA, adds a second lock on top of your password so that a leaked password alone is not enough for someone to get into your account. To sign in, an attacker would need both something you know (your password) and something you have (your phone or a security key).

Common types of 2FA

The most common form is a one-time code sent by text message or generated by an authenticator app, which you type in after your password. Some services instead send a push notification to your phone that you simply approve, and higher-security accounts can use a physical security key that you plug in or tap. Each method trades off convenience against security slightly differently, but all of them are far safer than a password on its own.

Why it is worth the extra step

2FA feels like friction the first time you set it up, but it only asks for that second step occasionally, and it is the single biggest thing you can add after choosing a strong password. Together, the three lessons in this tutorial cover the full picture: a strong, unique password for every account, a manager so you can actually keep that up, and 2FA as a backup in case a password ever gets out.

Quick checklist

  • Turn on 2FA for email first - it can be used to reset your other accounts.
  • Prefer an authenticator app or security key over text messages where possible.
  • Save the backup codes a service gives you somewhere safe, not just your phone.
